Rural vs Urban Healthcare: A High-Level Review

Physician shortages, longer distances and temporary emergency department closures particularly affect rural communities in Alberta. What the differences are and which resources can help.

Elmy Castillo — Rural Health Correspondent, County of Newell, Alberta

Many Canadians interact with the healthcare system every day. This may involve visiting a family physician for an annual checkup, seeking care at a walk-in clinic, or attending an urgent care centre or emergency department. However, how we access healthcare is often shaped by the communities in which we live. So, what does healthcare engagement look like in rural communities compared with urban centres, and what are some useful healthcare resources every Albertan should know?

In Canada, primary care serves as the first point of contact for patients seeking health services. Yet, rural communities frequently face shortages of primary care providers. Although approximately 18% of Canadians live in rural areas, only 8% of physicians practise in these communities. Rural residents also often face twice the travel time of their urban counterparts when accessing a physician or hospital. Currently, approximately 77% of rural residents are attached to a primary care provider, nearly 8 percentage points lower than the proportion of urban residents. Ideally, these numbers would be higher, as continuity of care with a family physician is associated with lower emergency department use. Conversely, limited or no continuity with a primary care provider is associated with increased emergency department visits.

Editor's note on the figures. The 18% rural population versus 8% of physicians matches Canadian Medical Association (2019) and CMAJ data, and the link between continuity of care and lower emergency department use is supported by a 2024 Alberta study. We could not confirm the 77% figure in an official source: according to Statistics Canada, in 2023 the share of people with a regular health care provider was 84.2% in rural communities and 82.5% in population centres, slightly higher in rural areas, although surveys use different definitions. On travel, available sources indicate rural distances to a physician or hospital are double or more; the exact travel-time figure remains to be confirmed.

Differences between rural and urban healthcare access extend beyond primary care. Studies have found that rural primary care providers (PCPs) make specialist referrals less frequently than their urban counterparts. This may reflect geographical barriers, including the distance patients must travel to receive specialist care, as well as limited specialist availability in rural communities. Given that specialist wait times are already lengthy across the healthcare system, these additional barriers can make accessing specialized care particularly challenging for rural residents.

In Alberta, temporary rural emergency department closures have further disrupted access to healthcare. These closures often occur when health facilities are unable to secure replacement physicians to cover planned or unexpected absences. When considered alongside the lower rates of primary care attachment among rural residents, these disruptions paint a concerning picture of healthcare accessibility in rural Alberta.

Addressing these challenges requires efforts at both the healthcare system and community levels. Local governments have introduced incentive packages to attract and, ideally, retain physicians in rural communities. However, improving access is not solely the responsibility of healthcare providers and policymakers. As patients, understanding the range of available healthcare resources and how to access them can also help us navigate the system more effectively. For example, residents can call 811 Health Link to speak with a registered nurse for health advice, consult pharmacists who can assess and prescribe treatments for eligible minor ailments, and proactively monitor chronic conditions to help prevent complications that may otherwise require acute or hospital-based care.

Ultimately, these strategies are relevant to both rural and urban residents. However, in communities where access to traditional healthcare services may be limited, knowing what resources are available and how to use them can make a meaningful difference.

Health resources in Alberta

  • Health Link 811 (Primary Care Alberta): free 24/7 health advice from a registered nurse.
  • Alberta Find a Provider: providers accepting new patients.
  • Estimated emergency department wait times (Alberta Health Services).

Important: 811 provides health advice but does not replace emergency services. In a medical emergency, call 911.
