 Overview
 Pneumococcal disease is caused by infection with the bacteria Streptococcus pneumoniae. Pneumococcal infection can lead to pneumonia (infection of the lungs), otitis media (infection of the middle ear) and meningitis (infection of the membranes around the brain and spinal cord).&nbsp;
\r\n Invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) is a serious bacterial infection commonly associated with severe respiratory tract infections. It is one of the leading causes of illness, hospitalization and death worldwide.&nbsp;
\r\n Early diagnosis is very important for serious pneumococcal infections. Your doctor or nurse practitioner can diagnose pneumococcal disease from the symptoms, doing an examination or through special tests. These tests may include a chest x-ray and/or taking samples to look for the bacteria in the infected part of your body.

 Symptoms
 Pneumococcal disease can include many different types of infections. Symptoms depend on the part of the body that is infected and the age of the person.&nbsp;
\r\n Pneumonia can cause shortness of breath, fever, lack of energy, loss of appetite, headache, chest pain and cough.
\r\n Otitis media in children can cause crying, tugging at the ear, fever, irritability, poor hearing and sometimes diarrhea and vomiting.
\r\n Meningitis can cause fever, headache, stiff neck, nausea, vomiting and drowsiness.

 Pneumococcal disease can include many different types of infections. Symptoms depend on the part of the body that is infected and the age of the person. 
 Pneumonia can cause shortness of breath, fever, lack of energy, loss of appetite, headache, chest pain and cough.
 Otitis media in children can cause crying, tugging at the ear, fever, irritability, poor hearing and sometimes diarrhea and vomiting.
 Meningitis can cause fever, headache, stiff neck, nausea, vomiting and drowsiness.
 How it spreads
 People spread pneumococcal bacteria to others through direct contact with respiratory secretions, like saliva or mucus. Many people, especially children, have the bacteria in their nose or throat at one time or another and don’t get sick; however, the bacteria sometimes can cause an infection.&nbsp;

 Risk factors
 In Canada, invasive pneumococcal disease is most common in the very young, individuals living with chronic illnesses or immune compromising conditions, individuals with certain lifestyle factors and adults over 65 years of age. Close contacts of positive cases are usually not at increased risk of infection, and if a follow up is required, individuals will be contacted by Public Health.&nbsp;
\r\n If you are unsure if your medical condition puts you at high risk, contact your primary care provider.

 Treatment 
 Treatment includes antibiotics, medicine to control the fever and pain and fluids to prevent dehydration.
\r\n Health-care providers and laboratories must report all cases of Invasive pneumococcal disease to Public Health. Public health staff will talk to the health-care provider and patient (or caregivers) to find out how the infection occurred, identify other people at risk of infection, suggest actions to help stop the spread (such as immunization) and provide other advice.&nbsp;

 Prevention
 Vaccines are available to prevent many types of pneumococcal disease. The New Brunswick Immunization Schedule provides pneumococcal vaccine for the following individuals:&nbsp;
\r\n \r\n all children at age two months, four months and 12 months of age
\r\n individuals aged 65 years and over who have never previously received a pneumococcal vaccine
\r\n individuals 18 years and over, newly admitted to long-term care facility
\r\n any individuals who have specific health and environmental conditions that place them at greater risk of pneumococcal disease
\r\n \r\n For additional information, contact your health-care provider, local Public Health office or Tele-Care 811 .
