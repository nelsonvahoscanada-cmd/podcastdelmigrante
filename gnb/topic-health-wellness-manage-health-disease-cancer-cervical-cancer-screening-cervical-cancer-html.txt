 Cervical cancer is preventable through HPV vaccination and when found early through screening.
 Regular cervical screening can help find problems on the cervix before cancer can develop. The human papillomavirus (HPV) test has replaced Pap as the main screening test for cervical cancer. 
 This cervical screening test (HPV) can identify those at risk for cervical precancer and cancer earlier because it detects the presence of the virus. 
 Nearly all cervical cancer cases are caused by high-risk infection. 
 Who should be screened for cervical cancer
 Women, trans men, or gender-diverse people aged 25-69 who have a cervix and have ever been sexually active (including any type of genital contact, with or without vaginal penetration) should have a cervical screening test (HPV) every five years.&nbsp;
\n If your last cervical screening was a Pap test and the result was normal (negative result), your next screening will be an HPV test in three years.&nbsp;
\n Those who have received the HPV vaccine or who are menopausal should also participate in cervical screening.&nbsp;&nbsp;
\n However, some people with special circumstances should be screened more frequently. These include:&nbsp;
\n \n people who have been treated for abnormal Pap, HPV tests or an invasive cervical cancer&nbsp;&nbsp;
\n people who have conditions that lower their immune response. For example, transplant recipients, HIV positive people and those treated with immune and/or biologic modifying agents&nbsp;

 Cervical screening - HPV tests
 The HPV test has replaced Pap as the primary screening test for cervical cancer.&nbsp;&nbsp;
\n This cervical screening test checks for the presence of high-risk types of the HPV virus known to cause almost all cervical cancers. HPV testing detects cervical precancer earlier and with greater accuracy.&nbsp;

 How HPV tests are collected
 An HPV cervical screening test is collected in the same way as a Pap test.&nbsp;A health-care provider will perform this test in their office or at a clinic.&nbsp;
\n First, the health-care provider will talk to you about cervical screening and how the HPV test will be done, then:&nbsp;
\n \n you will be asked to lie on an examination table with your feet in supports
\n a tool called a speculum is gently inserted into the vagina to hold it open so the cervix (the opening of the uterus or womb) can be clearly seen
\n they will collect a small sample from the cervix
\n \n The test only takes a few minutes to perform. The sample will be sent to the lab to be tested for HPV.&nbsp;
\n You will be contacted by your health-care provider with your results.&nbsp;

 When to start cervical screening
 If you have a cervix and have ever been sexually active (meaning intercourse, oral or touching of the genital area), you can begin cervical screening (HPV test) at the age of 25.&nbsp;&nbsp;
\n Routine cervical screening is NOT recommended if you are under the age of 25 (even if you have been sexually active).&nbsp;(Example: First sexual encounter at age 19 = first cervical screening test at age 25)&nbsp;
\n If your last cervical screening was a normal (negative) Pap test, your next screening will be an HPV test in three years.&nbsp;

 When to stop cervical screening
 If you are over the age of 69 and have had normal (negative) cervical screening (Pap or HPV tests) in the past ten years, you can probably stop having cervical screening tests. This can include any combination of negative Pap or HPV tests over the 10-year period.&nbsp;
\n However, you should talk to a primary health-care provider about cervical cancer screening if you are over the age of 69 and have never had a Pap or HPV test, do not remember when you had your last test, or have any symptoms such as abnormal bleeding.&nbsp;
\n If you are younger than 69 years of age and have stopped having your periods (menstrual cycles), then you should still complete cervical screening. Statistics show that cervical cancer cases occur more frequently in women over 50 years of age.&nbsp;

 Normal HPV result
 A normal or negative HPV test means the cells taken from your cervix show no presence of human papillomavirus - the virus which causes almost all cervical cancers. This means that your next routine cervical screening (HPV) test will be due in five years.
\n However, no screening test is perfect. If you ever experience symptoms of concern (i.e.: abnormal vaginal bleeding, pain during sexual intercourse or abnormal or persistent vaginal discharge), you should make an appointment to see a doctor or nurse practitioner.&nbsp;

 Abnormal HPV result
 An abnormal or positive cervical screening test result means that a type of human papillomavirus (HPV) was detected in the sample your doctor, nurse practitioner or healthcare provider collected from your cervix. When the result is positive for HPV, the lab will automatically do a Pap test (look for abnormal cell changes under a microscope) on the same sample.&nbsp;
\n HPV is a very common virus. Most of the time, HPV is not harmful and goes away on its own. But some types of HPV can cause changes to the cells on the cervix. If these changes are not checked or treated, they could lead to cervical cancer.&nbsp;
\n It is important to keep in mind that having HPV does not necessarily mean having cervical cancer. It just means that it is important that you contact the health care provider who collected your cervical screening test to discuss your test results and get the follow up testing required.&nbsp;
\n Follow up can include returning for another (repeat) cervical screening test or a referral to a specialist (gynecologist) who may perform further tests or treatment, such as colposcopy.&nbsp;&nbsp;

 New Brunswick Cervical Cancer Screening Program
 The New Brunswick Cervical Cancer Screening Program is dedicated to making sure that New Brunswick women, trans men and gender diverse people with an intact cervix aged 25 to 69 participate in regular cervical cancer screening, with an HPV test. This program is managed by the New Brunswick Cancer Network at the Department of Health, in partnership with health-care providers and the regional health authorities. Its purpose is to lower the rates of cervical cancer in New Brunswick by improving prevention, early detection and screening of this preventable disease.
\n The Cervical Program monitors screening activity (HPV and Pap tests) to determine who is screened and who is not screened within the province.
\n The program sends letters to individuals and health-care providers when screening or follow-up is overdue. This is to make sure that those who have not been participating in screening are invited or reminded and that those with significantly abnormal test results get the follow-up they need.
\n The program is an active member of the Pan-Canadian Cervical Cancer Screening Network, facilitated by the Canadian Partnership Against Cancer .

 Program letters
 As part of the New Brunswick Cervical Cancer Screening Program, letters will be sent to eligible New Brunswickers (age 25 to 69) and to primary health-care providers according to clinical practice guidelines.&nbsp;
\n The program will send:&nbsp;
\n \n invitation letters to newly eligible people (25 years of age or older or relocated to New Brunswick) or any eligible person between 25 to 69 years of age who has not had cervical screening (no record of a Pap or HPV test since 2011 or no record of a total hysterectomy since 2002 in New Brunswick)&nbsp;
\n rescreen reminder letters to eligible people who have been screened in the past but are overdue for routine cervical screening (more than three years since last Pap OR more than five years since last HPV test)&nbsp;

\n \n the cervical screening Pap test result is negative&nbsp;
\n the follow-up is done according to the Clinical Practice Guidelines for normal or abnormal results&nbsp;
\n people are outside the target age group of 25 to 69, without a cervix, already had a cervical cancer, or choose to ‘not participate’ (opt out) in the cervical program&nbsp;

 Colposcopy
 Follow-up after an abnormal test result often requires returning for another (repeat) cervical screening test.&nbsp;
\n Sometimes, you may need to be referred to a specialist (gynecologist) who may perform further tests and/or treatment such as a colposcopy.&nbsp;&nbsp;
\n A colposcopy is an exam of the cervix (the opening to the uterus or womb) done much the same way as a provider collects a cervical screening test.&nbsp;
\n A tool called a speculum is gently inserted into the vagina to hold it open so the cervix can be clearly seen and examined.&nbsp;&nbsp;
\n The colposcopy doctor (gynecologist) uses a special instrument called a colposcope. It has a light and magnifying lens (much like binoculars) on the end, which allows the inside surface of the cervix and the vagina to be seen more clearly under high magnification. The colposcope itself isn't inserted – it is positioned outside the opening of the vagina.&nbsp;
\n The area may be swabbed with solutions that help the doctor see the tissues better.&nbsp;
\n Biopsies may be taken from any suspicious areas using forceps or a scoop called a curette. This can feel like a cramp.&nbsp;
\n Once the biopsy specimens (when taken) have been examined by the lab, the colposcopy doctor will contact you with the results or an appointment.&nbsp;&nbsp;
\n The colposcopy doctor will return you to your primary health-care provider for cervical screening tests when they feel comfortable that your colposcopy examination is complete.&nbsp;

 Follow-up after an abnormal test result often requires returning for another (repeat) cervical screening test. 
 Sometimes, you may need to be referred to a specialist (gynecologist) who may perform further tests and/or treatment such as a colposcopy. 
 A colposcopy is an exam of the cervix (the opening to the uterus or womb) done much the same way as a provider collects a cervical screening test. 
 A tool called a speculum is gently inserted into the vagina to hold it open so the cervix can be clearly seen and examined. 
 The colposcopy doctor (gynecologist) uses a special instrument called a colposcope. It has a light and magnifying lens (much like binoculars) on the end, which allows the inside surface of the cervix and the vagina to be seen more clearly under high magnification. The colposcope itself isn't inserted – it is positioned outside the opening of the vagina. 
 The area may be swabbed with solutions that help the doctor see the tissues better. 
 Biopsies may be taken from any suspicious areas using forceps or a scoop called a curette. This can feel like a cramp. 
 Once the biopsy specimens (when taken) have been examined by the lab, the colposcopy doctor will contact you with the results or an appointment. 
 The colposcopy doctor will return you to your primary health-care provider for cervical screening tests when they feel comfortable that your colposcopy examination is complete. 
 Where to go for a colposcopy
 Your health-care provider (doctor or nurse practitioner) will refer you to a specialist (gynecologist) for colposcopy testing. Colposcopies are usually done in an outpatient clinic at one of the regional hospitals closest to your home. The gynecologist’s office or colposcopy clinic will send instructions to you on how to prepare for a colposcopy.

 After colposcopy, the specialist (gynecologist) will talk to you about your results.

 HPV and HPV vaccine
 HPV (Human Papillomavirus) is the most common sexually transmitted infection that is easily passed through close sexual contact, like touching or oral, vaginal, or anal sex. Nearly everyone who is sexually active and has not been vaccinated will be infected by HPV at some time in their lives without realizing it.&nbsp;&nbsp;
\n HPV infections often have no symptoms and most infections go away by themselves within two years. But sometimes, HPV infections can lead to several types of cancers, including cervical, oral pharyngeal, anal and penile.&nbsp;&nbsp;
\n Get vaccinated. The most effective way to lower your risks and protect yourself from HPV infections and related cancers is to get vaccinated.&nbsp;&nbsp;
\n More information about the HPV vaccination is available through Public Health .
