 Overview
 ​Tobacco is a plant used to make products that deliver nicotine to the body. Nicotine is the addictive drug in tobacco products, but they also contain many other chemicals that cause serious health problems.
\n Tobacco is prepared differently depending on how it will be consumed:
\n \n cigarettes use finely cut tobacco leaves rolled in thin paper
\n cigars use whole tobacco leaves
\n snuff is a finely ground powder that often comes in teabag-like pouches
\n chewing tobacco uses shredded tobacco leaves
\n hookah uses flavoured and sweetened tobacco that is heated, and smoke filters through water into a mouthpiece
\n \n The mood-altering effects of nicotine are subtle, complex and powerful. Some people feel that smoking helps them be more alert and concentrate, while others feel more relaxed. Smoking raises levels of a brain chemical called dopamine, which increases pleasure and reinforces the desire to continue smoking.
\n Vaping is the act of inhaling and exhaling an aerosol produced by a vaping product, such as an electronic cigarette. Vaping doesn’t require burning like cigarettes. The device heats liquid into a vapour, which then turns into an aerosol. This vapour can contain nicotine or cannabis, though all flavours besides tobacco have been banned in New Brunswick.

 Health impacts of tobacco
 Smoking and second-hand smoke irritates the eyes, nose and throat. Smoking also weakens the sense of taste and smell, reduces hunger and causes the stomach to produce acid.
\n Tobacco use remains Canada’s leading preventable cause of disease and death. It is a primary risk factor for several diseases that kill hundreds of New Brunswickers each year, including cancer, lung diseases and cardiovascular diseases. The World Health Organization estimates that tobacco kills up to half its users, making it one of the greatest public health threats. 
\n Emerging evidence shows that young people who use e-cigarettes are twice as likely to try smoking and almost twice as likely to be daily smokers by the following year.
\n Second-hand smoke is the smoke that comes from a burning tobacco product and the smoke exhaled. Many chemicals in tobacco are also found in second-hand smoke. Because of this, tobacco smoke may cause headaches, dizziness, nausea, coughing and wheezing and can aggravate allergies and asthma.&nbsp;
\n &nbsp;Key facts about second-hand smoke:
\n \n there is no safe level of exposure to second-hand smoke – even brief exposure can harm
\n people who do not smoke but live with someone who smokes have a 30% increased risk of lung cancer, heart disease and stroke
\n infants and children exposed to second-hand smoke have a higher risk of respiratory illnesses and ear infections
\n second-hand smoke can cause premature death and disease in children and adults who do not smoke

 Health impacts of vaping
 It will take several decades to realize the full extent of potential harm associated with vaping. Vapes almost always contain nicotine, which is highly addictive.
\n The current estimated harm associated with modern e-cigarettes or “vapes” is one third the harm associated with tobacco smoking for respiratory, cardiac and cancer-related illnesses.
\n However, less harmful does not mean safe. Smoking kills half of all people who smoke long-term.
\n Research conducted on vaping to date has highlighted some of the following potential risks:
\n \n vaping increases risk for respiratory disease (this includes asthma, bronchitis, emphysema and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or COPD)
\n e-cigarettes contain carcinogens – carcinogens are cancer causing substances
\n daily e-cigarette use is associated with increased odds of a heart attack
\n nicotine has been linked to worsened symptoms of mental illness
\n \n For youth, nicotine can interfere with healthy brain development. A person’s brain develops into their mid-20s and exposure to nicotine dramatically increases the risk of mood disorders, lower impulse control, disruption to brain circuits that control learning and lower emotional regulation.&nbsp;
\n Many people use e-cigarettes to cope with stress or anxiety. Emerging evidence shows a link between quitting vaping and improvements in mental health symptoms:
\n \n 90% of those who quit said they felt less stressed, anxious or depressed
\n 47% of those who quit reported feeling more in control of their life
\n 78% of respondents who had not quit said they would feel better about themselves if they quit
\n \n The use of vaping products outside the legal market, which are not subject to any oversight, pose additional risks. Do not modify vaping products or add any substances to these products that are not intended by the manufacturer.
