Acute Flaccid Paralysis (AFP)
 Symptoms, diagnosis, treatment and prevention of acute flaccid paralysis (AFP).

 Symptoms, diagnosis, treatment and prevention of acute flaccid paralysis (AFP).
 On this page
 Overview
 AFP is a set of symptoms, not a disease or diagnosis. AFP is defined as the sudden onset of muscle weakness or paralysis in children younger than 15 years old, that is not due to trauma. In Canada, AFP symptoms are reported and investigated as a way to rapidly identify or rule out poliomyelitis (polio).&nbsp;
\r\n AFP may be caused by polio or a number of other agents. The most common cause of AFP in Canada is Guillain Barré Syndrome. Some other causes of AFP include viral or bacterial infections, autoimmune disorders, or environmental toxin exposure. Sometimes the cause is unknown.
\r\n This is why there is no specific test for AFP. Diagnostic testing aims to identify the agent causing the illness and to rule out or confirm polio. A health-care provider begins by obtaining a client history and timeline of symptoms. Lab testing may involve stool samples, blood samples, throat swabs, spinal cord fluid or neurologic imaging scans.

 Symptoms
 The most common symptom is sudden onset of weakness or flaccid paralysis, where the affected muscle group is limp and does not contract or spasm. AFP arising from paralytic polio does not affect both sides of the body equally; some limbs or muscle groups are affected and others are not. Fever is usually present at onset. Flaccid paralysis can also affect muscles of the head, neck or the diaphragm muscle that is used for breathing.

 Treatment
 Treatment depends on what is causing AFP.&nbsp;
\r\n Health-care providers, hospitals and laboratories, schools and childcare centres must report suspected cases of AFP to Public Health. Public Health staff will interview the health-care provider and patient (or caregivers) to help determine the underlying cause of the AFP. If due to certain communicable illnesses, Public Health will find out how the infection occurred, identify other people at risk of infection, implement control measures (such as immunization and restrictions on attending school or work) and provide other advice.&nbsp;

Public Health staff will interview the health-care provider and patient (or caregivers) to help determine the underlying cause of the AFP. If due to certain communicable illnesses, Public Health will find out how the infection occurred, identify other people at risk of infection, implement control measures (such as immunization and restrictions on attending school or work) and provide other advice. 
 Prevention
 Prevention may vary according to the underlying cause of AFP. In general, individuals can reduce their risk by using healthy habits to prevent communicable illness, including:
\r\n \r\n frequent handwashing
\r\n avoiding touching their face with their hands
\r\n coughing and sneezing into their arm or a tissue, not their hands
\r\n using safe food handling practices&nbsp;
\r\n keeping common surface areas clean, such as countertops
\r\n staying current with the New Brunswick immunization schedule and vaccines recommended before travel abroad
\r\n remaining aware of health notices for Canadians traveling abroad and seeking medical care if unwell after international travel
\r\n avoiding close contact with people who are sick and staying home when sick
\r\n \r\n For additional information, contact your health-care provider, local Public Health office or Tele-Care 811.
