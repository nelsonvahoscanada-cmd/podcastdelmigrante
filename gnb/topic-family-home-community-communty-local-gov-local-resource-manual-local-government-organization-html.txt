 Councillors can be elected on a ward-based system, an at-large system or a combination of the two. The mayor is always elected on an at-large basis. 
 A council can determine, through adoption of a by-law, the number of council members and how representation will be determined (at large, by ward or a combination of both). 
 Vacancies on council can occur for a variety of reasons and specific steps must be taken by the local government to address vacancies when they occur. 
 There are different ways local governments may be organized to conduct their business. Two approaches commonly used include the Council-CAO system and the Council-Standing Committee system and there are benefits and challenges with each. 
 The purposes, powers, and functions of local governments are outlined in the Local Governance Act . 
 Roles and responsibilities of council and senior staff are generally outlined in the legislation and understanding them is critical to the effective functioning of a local government. 
 There are a variety of actions a council and staff can take to help ensure a healthy and effective local government team is established and maintained.
 Part A — Representation on a Local Government Council 
 3.1 Methods of Election
 Sections 42 through 47 of the Local Governance Act outline the legislative authority for establishing council composition (number of council members) and the type of system by which council members will be elected – (i.e., by general vote – the “at large” system or by voting based on wards, or a combination of the two).&nbsp;

 3.2 Council Representation
 a. Ward System
 A local government is divided into sections (wards) by a by-law of its council or through the Local Governments Establishment Regulation that provides a detailed description of the boundaries of each individual ward. The wards are usually based on criteria such as size and population, with one or more council members representing each ward. The council member elected to represent a ward must reside in that ward at the time of nomination. The residents of that ward vote for the candidate of their choice. When there is only one candidate nominated for a seat in a ward, that candidate is elected by acclamation.&nbsp;&nbsp;
\r\n Although a council member is elected to represent a particular ward in this system, they must nonetheless consider the interests of the entire community when engaged in the decision-making process. The council of a local government divided into wards may, where a by-law so provides, also have one or more councillors elected on an at-large basis. In a ward only system, the mayor is the only candidate who is elected at large by all voters in the local government.&nbsp;

 b. At-large system
 In the case of an “at-large” system, all candidates who run for council are elected by eligible voters of the entire community. All eligible voters may cast their votes for their preferred candidates running for the allotted council seats and for the mayoral position. Those candidates who receive the highest number of votes win the available seats on the council.&nbsp;

 c. Changing from an “at-large” system to a “ward” system or vice versa
 To change from an “at-large” system to a “ward” system, the following procedure is to be followed:&nbsp;
\r\n \r\n Staff from the local government prepares analysis of options for changing system of local representation for consideration by council.&nbsp;
\r\n Council passes a by-law dividing the local government into specified wards.&nbsp; The by-law requires an affirmative vote of a majority of the council. Note that this is a higher threshold than required for typical council resolutions and by-law making decisions. Normally, such decisions require a majority of the members present, as a long as there is a quorum.&nbsp;
\r\n The by-law must be filed with the Municipal Electoral Officer more than six months before the next general election (to have effect for that election).&nbsp;
\r\n This by-law cannot be repealed or amended until four years have elapsed from the making or amendment of the by-law or six months preceding a municipal election.&nbsp;
\r\n \r\n The same process would be used to move from a “ward” system to an “at- large” system and all items noted above also apply.&nbsp;

 d. Elected to represent a ward — moves out of the ward
 In a local government that is divided into wards for election purposes, a person is not qualified to be nominated as a candidate for a ward unless they are a resident of that ward when they are nominated.&nbsp;
\r\n If a ward councillor moves out of the ward they represent but remains within the boundaries of the local government, they can continue to serve as a council member.&nbsp;

 e. Advantages and disadvantages: ward vs. at-large systems
 The type of electoral system a council chooses may depend upon the demographics and culture of the community and the history of the area.&nbsp;
\r\n The at-large system may appeal to smaller communities, as smaller populations and boundary areas may not be suitable for division into wards. Moreover, it is not always easy to find candidates to run for municipal office, let alone for specific wards.&nbsp;&nbsp;
\r\n A ward system might be more suitable where a local government is significantly larger in population and area as well as diverse in its history, culture, race and ethnicity.&nbsp; This may help to ensure that all interests are more adequately represented on the council.&nbsp;
\r\n A hybrid approach of including both ward-based representation and councillors elected at large in a community may be an effective compromise for some communities. It may provide further assurance that both the broad and specific interests of the community are represented and considered.&nbsp;

 3.3 Council Composition
 Council composition will vary among local governments. The composition of a council shall be determined by by-law. ( Local Governance Act : 42, 43, 44)&nbsp;
\r\n The minimum number of council members elected in a local government shall consist of a mayor and three councillors.&nbsp; A by-law that changes the composition of the council can only be passed with the approval of a majority of the council. Once passed, it cannot be amended or repealed for four years. The process of changing council composition is the same as it is for by-laws to change from an at-large system to a ward system or vice versa.&nbsp;
\r\n It is important to note that the “majority of the whole” includes all members of council including the mayor, (e.g. if you have six councillors and a mayor, the entire council would be seven, and a majority of four votes would be needed to pass this by-law).  Where a local government’s procedural by-law specifies that “a mayor shall only vote in the event of a tie”, the quorum of council remains seven, and four votes would be required for a decision in this example.  ( Local Governance Act : 43, 44, 45.)&nbsp;
\r\n There are some by-laws that require a larger threshold such as the composition of council by-law.&nbsp; The Act will state if a higher threshold is required.&nbsp;

 3.4 Acclamation
 Whether candidates run for office in their local government in an “at-large” system, ward system, or combination of both, if there are no more candidates nominated than are required for the council positions, the candidates are deemed elected by acclamation. For example, in an at-large system, if there are five council seats (four councillors and mayor) and there are five candidates (four candidates for councillor positions and one candidate for the mayor position) then all candidates would be acclaimed, and they would form the new council. ( Municipal Elections Act : 19(1).)&nbsp;

 3.5 Vacancy on Council 
 When does a vacancy occur?&nbsp; 
\n A vacancy on council occurs for the following reasons:&nbsp;
\n \n There are fewer candidates nominated than are required for council office.&nbsp;
\n A member resigns from office.&nbsp;
\n A member dies while in office.&nbsp;
\n A member is convicted of an offence punishable by imprisonment for five or more years, or an offence under section 122, 123, 124 or 125 of the Criminal Code of Canada.&nbsp;
\n A member fails to take and subscribe to the oath of office prescribed by the Minister after the expiration of the time frame referred to in section 42(1) of the Municipal Elections Act following the person’s election.&nbsp;
\n A member ceases to be a resident of the local government.&nbsp;
\n Except in the case of illness or authorization of a leave by the council (resolution required for authorization), a member is absent from the local government for more than two months at one time or absent from four or more consecutive regular meetings of the council.&nbsp;
\n A member has been disqualified or declared incapable of holding office under this or any other Act. ( Local Governance Act : 50(1).)&nbsp;
\n \n Important steps for vacancy declaration&nbsp; 
\n \n A member of council may resign from office by filing with the clerk a resignation in writing .&nbsp; &nbsp;
\n When a member of council resigns, dies while holding office or is no longer able to serve on council for a reason outlined in subsection 50(1) of the Local Governance Act , the council must within two months from the date the vacancy occurred , declare the vacancy by a resolution of council.&nbsp;&nbsp;
\n Within 10 days of the declaration of the vacancy by council resolution, the clerk of the local government forwards a certified copy of the resolution to the Municipal Electoral Officer at Elections NB by email at info@electionsnb.ca or to the following address:&nbsp;

 3.6 Types of local government structures
 The general principle of the responsibilities of elected officials and local administrators and the type of structure a local government adopts to function includes a separation of governance and administration. Council members are elected to provide direction, and the administration implements the direction of council.&nbsp;
\r\n"}}" id="text-28c22091c5" class="cmp-text">
 The general principle of the responsibilities of elected officials and local administrators and the type of structure a local government adopts to function includes a separation of governance and administration. Council members are elected to provide direction, and the administration implements the direction of council. 
 a. Council-chief administrative officer (CAO) system 
 The council-chief administrative officer (CAO) system is based on the principle of separation of policy from administration. In general terms, a council is responsible for setting policy direction for the local government, while staff, through the CAO, is responsible for administration and operations. Council must not be involved in the day-to-day administration and operations of the local government.&nbsp;&nbsp;
\n The CAO of a local government is expected to:&nbsp;
\n \n ensure that council policies and decisions are adhered to and carried out
\n supervise department heads/staff
\n coordinate, develop and implement hiring and human resource management practices
\n oversee the preparation of annual estimates (budget) for the council’s budget deliberations
\n oversee the administration and enforcement of by-laws
\n prepare and present reports to council
\n apprise the council of all matters of importance in the community
\n apprise the council on all matters of importance regarding administration of the local government
\n ensure the local government has developed and maintained a Capital Asset Management plan
\n ensure the local government has adopted an annual report each year
\n work with the council to develop a strategic plan for the local government and follow up with the plan implementation
\n \n Some of the benefits of having a council–CAO organizational structure include:&nbsp;
\n \n ensuring sound and balanced advice to councils
\n ensuring that staff report to one individual and not to several members of council
\n ensuring that reports and recommendations are thorough and prepared before being considered by council for a decision
\n helping to ensure greater staff stability, particularly following local government elections
\n enabling the council to focus on its primary role of policy setting, providing strategic direction and related decision-making for the local government
\n ensuring that there are systems in place for the ongoing training, professional development and compensation of staff
\n ensuring that effective and efficient administrative and operational policies and procedures are in place and maintained
\n \n All CAOs must ensure that they keep their respective councils informed of important administrative and operational matters. This can be done during council meetings.&nbsp; &nbsp;

 b. Committee of the Whole system (entire council sitting in one committee)
 In recent years, a trend has seen local governments have the entire membership of council sit in “Committee of the Whole” to improve efficiency (instead of having four or five standing committees). A Committee of the Whole provides the opportunity for all council members to hold discussions on local government matters and receive detailed information from administration, delegations or subject matter experts before it is formally considered and debated at a regular council meeting. This approach may help all council members be more informed of and more engaged in the issues of the local government.&nbsp;
\n Committee of the Whole meetings are, in a way, a council’s research and information session, and are held in a less formal atmosphere than a regular council meeting.&nbsp; &nbsp;
\n Any items presented at the Committee of the Whole that require formal resolutions of council (i.e., a decision) would be brought forward to a regular council meeting for consideration. As a best practice, the clerk and CAO should attend these sessions along with any other required staff, depending upon the matters being discussed. However, unlike the standing committee structure described below, the Committee of the Whole generally meets less frequently, and all council members are present to hear the discussion. This model may reduce the number of meetings for council members and may also reduce administrative expenses.&nbsp;
\n All Committee of the Whole meetings are to be open to the public. However, they may be closed to the public for the specific reasons listed in section 68 of the Local Governance Act .&nbsp; &nbsp;
\n It should also be noted that along with the Committee of the Whole structure, a council may still have standing or ad hoc committees. Examples may include committees, boards, or commissions established for recreation, economic development, and utility purposes.&nbsp;&nbsp;

 c. Council — standing committee system 
 Standing committees are working committees of council where recommendations are developed for council decision-making. The chair is appointed by the mayor (as determined in a local government’s procedural by-law) with two or three members of council on the committee. The committee appointees are ratified by a resolution of council, with position, mandate of the committee, and length of term stipulated in the procedural by-law.&nbsp; &nbsp;
\r\n While this structure is still in place in several local governments throughout the province, it is becoming less popular due to lack of resources and time commitment requirements. This type of structure tends to allow the bulk of discussion and debate of pertinent issues within various committees, with all recommendations forwarded to council for consideration and final decision-making. This being the case, there is the potential for some duplication of effort. Furthermore, local government staff may spend considerable time preparing for the various committees, sometimes at the expense of the overall administration of their respective departments.&nbsp;
\r\n The CAO, treasurer and clerk (or their designate) and staff responsible for the departments should be present at all standing committee meetings to provide advice on all administrative and financial matters so that well-informed and rational policy recommendations are put forward for council consideration. Examples of standing committees include those dealing with finances, recreation, public works, public safety, culture and heritage, human resources and economic development and growth.&nbsp;
\r\n Some local governments allow residents to participate on their standing committees. Ideally, qualifications and terms of reference for committee membership should be established and then residents can apply for the committee positions. Following a review of the applications, appointments to the committees can be formally made by resolutions of council.&nbsp; &nbsp;
\r\n To avoid other council members feeling that decisions have already been made prior to reaching the council table for ratification, it is best that the number of members of council on a standing committee does not exceed the council majority.&nbsp;
\r\n The mayor may participate as an ex-officio member of all standing committees.&nbsp;

 3.7 Ad Hoc Committees
 Ad hoc committees in a local government serve in an advisory capacity to council and are created by the council and should have well-defined mandates, duties and terms of reference. These committees, usually involving community-minded volunteers, are created as needs are identified and specific advice, research and recommendations are required for council to make well-informed decisions.&nbsp; Generally, the mandate and terms of reference are not identified in a local government’s procedural by-law, unlike the standing committees of council.&nbsp;

 Part C — Functions and Powers of Local Governments
 3.8 Purposes and Powers of the Local Government
 The Local Governance Act that came into force in 2018 recognizes the need to provide local governments with greater flexibility regarding their role in meeting the needs of their respective communities and residents. The purposes and powers of local governments were written and enacted with this in mind.&nbsp;
\r\n"}}" id="text-26a543760d" class="cmp-text">
 The Local Governance Act that came into force in 2018 recognizes the need to provide local governments with greater flexibility regarding their role in meeting the needs of their respective communities and residents. The purposes and powers of local governments were written and enacted with this in mind. 
 a. The purposes of a local government
 Local governments have been established for the following purposes:&nbsp;
\n \n Providing good government: this means that local councils and administrations are working together and making and implementing decisions aimed at supporting the positive development of their respective communities.&nbsp;
\n Providing services, facilities or things the council considers necessary or desirable for all or part of the local government.&nbsp;
\n Developing and maintaining safe and viable communities.&nbsp;
\n Fostering economic, social, and environmental well-being of their communities.&nbsp;
\n \n Combined, these purposes give local governments a broad mandate to serve, support and administer their communities. ( Local Governance Act : 5)&nbsp;
\n"}}" id="text-0502437ef4" class="cmp-text">
 Local governments have been established for the following purposes: 
 Providing good government: this means that local councils and administrations are working together and making and implementing decisions aimed at supporting the positive development of their respective communities.

 Developing and maintaining safe and viable communities. 
 Fostering economic, social, and environmental well-being of their communities. 
 Combined, these purposes give local governments a broad mandate to serve, support and administer their communities. ( Local Governance Act : 5) 
 b. The powers of a local government 
 To fulfill the purposes previously described, local governments have at their disposal a variety of powers aimed at giving them enough authority to meet the current and emerging needs of their communities. These include:&nbsp;

The council is the decision-making body for the local government; however, the legislation also gives certain staff of the local government various responsibilities and also allows councils to delegate some of their powers, duties and functions, with some exceptions, to officers of the local government.&nbsp;
\n Continuity of business: Anything begun by one council may be continued or completed by a succeeding council. This ensures that following local government elections, the work of the local government continues, and that decisions and initiatives of former councils continue to have effect until such time as council makes decisions to make adjustments.&nbsp;
\n Entering into agreements for providing services outside boundaries: Local governments may enter into agreements to provide services, utilities and facilities to persons outside the territorial limits of the local government.&nbsp;
\n Airport Commissions: A local government may participate in an airport commission and may enter into an agreement for that purpose. ( Local Governance Act : 6.)&nbsp;

Councillors should therefore be continuously thinking about what their local government provides and what adjustments, if any, should be made to better serve the community. ( Local Governance Act : 48(6)(a), (b).)

\n It should be noted that some local governments choose other titles for the CAO (e.g., City Manager).  e. Liaison with the public
 The CAO acts as the main liaison between the council and the residents of the local government.&nbsp; They (and / or other designated staff) may meet with the public on any matters concerning by-laws, policies or procedures that have been established by the council, as well as programs and services provided to and involving the community.
\n This could be an everyday occurrence with the CAO and will include meetings with residents who may or may not approve of a recent council decision. It is important that the CAO listens to the resident’s concerns and provides the relevant background information that would assist in clarifying the issue.&nbsp;

 3.20 Role of the Auditor
 The annual budget process in every local government is followed by an annual audit of the previous year. The auditor will be appointed by council by resolution and must be a chartered professional accountant or a certified general accountant. Their job is to provide independent assurance to council and the public that the local government is appropriately managing public resources and effectively delivering public services. The auditor is expected to perform such duties as prescribed by the regulations under the Local Governance Act , or that the council prescribes with a by-law or resolution. &nbsp;&nbsp;
\n The auditor must complete the local government’s annual audit by the first day of April and within 10 days of completing it, they must transmit to the Minister of Local Government a certified copy of the financial statements of the local government together with a copy of the Auditor’s Report on the financial statements. A council must appoint an auditor and if they fail to do so, the Minister may appoint one for them and the council shall pay the fees and expenses of the appointed auditor. ( Local Governance Act : 79.)&nbsp;

 3.21 Role of the Solicitor
 It should be clarified that the municipal solicitor is not the personal lawyer for either the individually elected or the appointed officials.  a. Overview
 A healthy relationship between the council and staff of a local government is essential for the effectiveness of a local government. This interaction is an aspect of local government operations that can be challenging at times and requires attention. This section will focus on the elements of a healthy council-staff relationship, potential challenges and what can be done to encourage positive working relations between council members and between council and the staff of a local government. Emphasis will be placed on the link between the senior administration (CAO / clerk) and the council.&nbsp;

 A healthy relationship between the council and staff of a local government is essential for the effectiveness of a local government. This interaction is an aspect of local government operations that can be challenging at times and requires attention. This section will focus on the elements of a healthy council-staff relationship, potential challenges and what can be done to encourage positive working relations between council members and between council and the staff of a local government.  b. A team effort
 It’s imperative that the relationship between the council and staff of a local government is one where there is mutual understanding and respect of roles and responsibilities, open two-way communication, and accepted reporting relationships, as well as clear direction from the elected body. Moreover, the importance of placing an emphasis on a team approach cannot be overstated.
\n There must be a willingness between council members and staff to trust one another, to build respect by listening to one another’s views, to share credit for good ideas, to keep each other informed on community issues and to work through solutions together. A council must respect staff’s role to provide the best advice and assistance while staff must respect council’s role to make decisions on behalf of the community that elected them.
\n Strong working relationships foster trust and goodwill, helping to promote a positive image for the community. When elected and staff collaborate effectively, the electorate can clearly see a shared commitment to serving the best interests of the entire community. While this level of collaboration takes effort, it is achievable. By taking intentional steps, both council members and senior administration can foster a relationship that is productive, constructive, and consistently responsive to the community’s needs.

 3.24 Planning sessions
 In the first year of its mandate, the council and administration could consider engaging in a general planning session. Such a session would provide an opportunity to identify priorities for the local government, examine possible changes to local government policies, by-laws, operations, structures and services, and can assist in identifying potential amendments to by-laws. In addition, particular services could be examined, if necessary (e.g., recreation, fire service, public works). Planning sessions can also be used to review the financial situation of the local government, with a view to the annual budget development and adoption process.&nbsp;
\r\n This type of planning session should provide an opportunity for the local government organization to establish priorities for the year and for the longer term. It can also serve as a forum through which a common vision or direction can be established not only between the council and the administration but also among the council members themselves. It may also serve as an opportunity for council members and the senior administration of a local government to share and discuss ideas in a more relaxed setting with a view to teambuilding.  It may be helpful to involve a third party to take the team through a formal strategic planning process to ensure greater objectivity and to learn about the experiences of other organizations.&nbsp;

 3.25 Suggestions for Newly Elected Officials
 a. Role recognition
 As an elected official, there are things that can be done to strengthen the relationship that exists between the administration and the council. First and foremost, elected officials must value and respect the role that the administration has in running the day-to-day operations of the local government. The experience and knowledge of administrators should be considered as assets to the local government that the council can rely upon in the decision-making process. It must be recognized that local government administrators (CAO, clerk, treasurer, department heads and others) provide continuity in the system and without them, the work of the local government would not be accomplished. It is also important to note that many administrators live in the local government and its success and growth reflect not only on the elected officials but on those who work for the local government as well.&nbsp;

 e. Letting the administrators do their jobs
 Council members must let the administration do its job. Getting involved in the day-to-day administration inevitably creates challenges.  c. Restrictions of powers of an outgoing council
 From the day of a general election and ending on the first meeting of council, the outgoing council continues to exercise its powers in relation to day-to-day activities of the local government. However, there are some restrictions as follows.&nbsp;&nbsp;
\n An outgoing council shall not:&nbsp;
\n \n make, amend or repeal a by-law under the authority of this or any other Act
\n become a party to any agreement, contract, instrument or any other document other than those provided for in the estimates adopted under paragraph 99(2)(a) for the current year
\n borrow or make payments of funds other than those provided for in the estimates adopted under paragraph 99(2)(a) for the current year
\n purchase or dispose of capital assets
\n appoint or dismiss officers or employees
\n \n An outgoing council may only act on the above-noted matters if they are in the public interest and are urgently required or authorized by a by-law made before the day of the general election .  a. Potential sources of conflict
 It is important for council members and senior staff to recognize that conflict may arise and to determine the potential sources. If the source of conflict (or potential conflict) can be recognized at an early stage, the matter can be dealt with in a proactive manner rather than letting what may be a minor problem become a major issue.&nbsp;

 b. Resolving a conflict
 What can be done to resolve a conflict when it arises? 
\n One approach is to address the matter immediately, particularly if the issue does not appear to be significant. A constructive discussion between the affected parties, preferably with a non-partisan facilitator, can assist in reaching the root of the conflict, which may in turn, lead to finding a mutually acceptable solution to the situation.&nbsp;
\n When there is mutual understanding and communication lines are open between the affected parties, any misunderstandings, personal slights, or power struggles can be identified and consequently reduced. The willingness of members of council and local government staff to reason and cooperate with one another leads to the development of positive, goal-oriented working relationships. Even if there is an agreement to ‘agree to disagree’ with one another, it is more constructive than constant disruptions at the council table and in the workplace.&nbsp;
\n What can be done if the conflict can’t be resolved? 
\n The local government may wish to consider mediation when a conflict can’t be overcome. The Department of Post-Secondary Education, Training and Labour (PETL) may have mediation services or tools available to assist. Industrial Relations Services at PETL can be contacted for further information.&nbsp;

 c. Relationships among council members
 In most instances new councils are a blend of seasoned and first-time council members. The formation of an effective council for a four-year term is a challenge, particularly for the newly elected and the CAO/Clerk. If the mayor is seasoned it may be beneficial to have the mayor meet individually with each new member of the council, to gain an understanding of their goals and vision for the community.&nbsp;&nbsp;
\n While there may be opposing views among council members, a council should see itself as a team that is working for the betterment of the entire community. It is therefore important that council members respect one another’s differences of opinion when a matter is being debated, and in the end try to come to a compromise. Once the final vote is taken, council members must respect the decision that is made by the whole of council. In the Canadian democracy, the majority rules, and elected officials are reminded that these rules must be followed.&nbsp;
\n During a council debate, the elected members should be coached not to interrupt while their colleagues have the floor. Common courtesy must be upheld Rules of order, such as those found in Robert’s Rules of Order, Bourinot’s or Code Morin should be followed and good conduct should be understood by all members of council. This is also a very important aspect during the orientation of council and should be emphasized. A local government’s procedural by-law will identify the rules of order that is adopted for meeting procedures.&nbsp;&nbsp;
\n If a council does become divided, it may impact the relationship with administration and with the community itself. Local government senior officers may find themselves caught in the middle of debates between the two sides. As much as possible, members of council must focus their attention on their mandate, which is to serve the community and to make decisions that serve the public’s best interests.&nbsp;
\n Group voting on council can be detrimental to a local government organization and the community in the long run. Deep divisions on council become apparent to the public. Again, the mayor as chair must play a strong leadership role to ensure that order is maintained, that everyone at the council table has a chance to be heard, and that established council procedures as detailed in the local government’s procedural by-law are respected.&nbsp; &nbsp;
