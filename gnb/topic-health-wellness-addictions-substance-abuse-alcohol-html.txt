 Overview
 Did you know that alcohol use is a leading cause of disease, disability, and premature death in Canada, even though those harms are preventable? The short-term health risks of alcohol use can include injuries, violence, and alcohol poisoning. Alcohol use also increases risk for serious long-term health issues,&nbsp;such as:&nbsp;cancer, liver disease, heart disease, stroke, mental illness, and alcohol dependence. In 2020, the health, social, and legal costs of alcohol consumption in New Brunswick totaled&nbsp; $411 million .

 Alcohol use in New Brunswick
 \r\n In 2022, the rate of heavy drinking in New Brunswick (18.9%) was significantly higher than the national average (15.6%).
\r\n In 2021, in New Brunswick, there was a significant difference in heavy drinking between males and females: 24% of males reported heavy drinking compared to 14.1% of females. It should be noted the health risks increase more steeply for females than for males after six standard drinks per week.
\r\n Data from the 2021-2022&nbsp; New Brunswick Student Wellness Survey &nbsp;showed that: 45% of youth in grades 6 to 12 reported having tried alcohol, 17.8% reported regular drinking, and 11.6% reported heavy drinking. The average age of onset (first trying alcohol) was 14 years old.

 Lowering your risk
 Canada’s Guidance on Alcohol and Health &nbsp;provides people living in Canada with&nbsp;important information&nbsp;about alcohol and health, allowing them to assess their personal risk and consider their alcohol use. With this guidance, we see that even&nbsp;small amounts&nbsp;of alcohol are not good for health:
\r\n \r\n There is a continuum of risk associated with weekly alcohol use where the risk of harm is: \r\n 0 drinks per week —&nbsp; Not drinking has benefits, such as better health, and better sleep.
\r\n 2 standard drinks or less per week —&nbsp; You are likely to avoid alcohol-related consequences for yourself or others at this level.
\r\n 3–6 standard drinks per week —&nbsp; Your risk of developing several types of cancer, including breast and colon cancer, increases at this level.
\r\n 7&nbsp;standard&nbsp;drinks or more per week —&nbsp; Your risk of heart disease or stroke increases significantly at this level.
\r\n \r\n
\r\n Consuming more than&nbsp;two&nbsp;standard drinks per occasion is associated with an increased risk of harm to self and others, including injuries and violence.
\r\n \r\n Pregnancy and breastfeeding
\r\n When pregnant or trying to get pregnant, there is no known safe amount of alcohol use. When breastfeeding, not drinking alcohol is safest.

 Canada’s Guidance on Alcohol and Health provides people living in Canada with important information about alcohol and health, allowing them to assess their personal risk and consider their alcohol use. With this guidance, we see that even small amounts of alcohol are not good for health:
 There is a continuum of risk associated with weekly alcohol use where the risk of harm is: 0 drinks per week — Not drinking has benefits, such as better health, and better sleep.
 2 standard drinks or less per week — You are likely to avoid alcohol-related consequences for yourself or others at this level.
 3–6 standard drinks per week — Your risk of developing several types of cancer, including breast and colon cancer, increases at this level.
 7 standard drinks or more per week — Your risk of heart disease or stroke increases significantly at this level.
 Consuming more than two standard drinks per occasion is associated with an increased risk of harm to self and others, including injuries and violence.
 Pregnancy and breastfeeding
 When pregnant or trying to get pregnant, there is no known safe amount of alcohol use. When breastfeeding, not drinking alcohol is safest.

 Signs of harmful drinking
 When alcohol use begins to interfere with the things that matter most to an individual, such as relationships, responsibilities, or personal goals, it may signal a risk of harm or the potential for dependence.&nbsp;In these situations, compassionate support is available, and individuals have the opportunity to choose approaches that respect their values, priorities, and unique circumstances.
\r\n When someone becomes&nbsp;physically dependent on&nbsp;alcohol, their body builds tolerance. This means they need to drink more to feel the same effects. Over time, physical dependence develops. If the person suddenly stops drinking, withdrawal symptoms can occur.
\r\n Common withdrawal symptoms&nbsp;may&nbsp;include&nbsp;trouble sleeping, shaking, nausea, and sometimes seizures. These symptoms can last anywhere from two to seven days and may be mild or severe, depending on how much and how long the person has been drinking.
\r\n In some cases, people experience a serious condition called delirium tremens (DTs) about five or six days after quitting. DTs can cause hallucinations, confusion, fever, and a rapid heartbeat. If left untreated, severe withdrawal can be life-threatening.&nbsp;
\r\n Anyone who wants to reduce or stop drinking should seek medical advice. Quitting alcohol suddenly without professional help can be dangerous.&nbsp;

 Our initiatives to reduce alcohol use
 The Department of Health has launched a five-year pilot project to reduce substance use, including alcohol, and related harms in youth, through the implementation of Planet Youth in four sites across New Brunswick. Planet Youth focuses on creating healthy community environments to support youth health and well-being. Planet Youth was developed based on the Icelandic Prevention Model, which has proven to reduce substance use in Iceland for over 20 years.
\r\n The Interdepartmental Addiction and Mental Health Action Plan (PDF 648 KB) provided a framework to guide and align visions, plans, and priorities for 2021–2025, as well as to foster collaboration among stakeholders and partners in mental health. 
\r\n This plan was aligned with the three main goals of the Department of Health’s Strategic Plan. The department is now developing a new plan to build on this work and address emerging priorities.
